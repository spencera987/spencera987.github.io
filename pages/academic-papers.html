A Technical Study Demonstrating the Poor Marketability of Near-Sonic Ultra-Long-Haul-Range Airliners

Abstract:

The ongoing conflict in Ukraine has closed off portions of European and Asian airspace causing aircraft to fly around these closures increasing flight times. Airline alliances like Star Alliance and the Middle East 3 fly many of the kinds of flights affected by the conflict. In the past, Boeing considered near-sonic flight as a configuration that could alleviate the issue of increased flight times for these airlines. Near-sonic flight presents its own issues, and the following paper will analyze the conceptual designs that form from the airline requirements for legal compliance and that the designs meet the needs of the airline. In addition to reviewing the publicly released specifications of the Boeing “Sonic Cruiser,” we synthesized our own designs using a MDO method. To ensure that all possible corners of the design space were covered, two types of aircraft engine configurations were analyzed: integrated and podded. We found that neither engine configuration led to a marketable design: near-sonic flight does not have enough time savings on short-haul flights and does not meet the economic and legal thresholds to function even with the increased flight times.

An Analysis of the Aerodynamic Performance of Formula One Racing Vehicles using a Tow Tank

Abstract:

The objectives of this project are to design a statically determinant load cell mechanism for a prototype tow tank ultimately culminating in the testing of the aerodynamic performance of a Formula One racing car model. This paper also serves as a proof of concept for force data collection for a full-sized tow tank being developed by Isabella All [8]. The project includes the design and construction of the load cell mechanism which utilizes a load cell to measure the force in a specific member of the mechanism which is then used to determine the semi-lift and drag forces for a given test model. For this specific project, a model of the front-end of an F1 racing car was used for data collection and analysis. It was found that for a short period of time within each test run, constant force data was able to be collected from the load cell which could then be transformed into semi-lift and drag force data. Ultimately, the drag coefficient acting on the model was found to be in the range of 0.9 to 1.3 which somewhat falls in line with the estimated values of 0.7 to 1.0 [1] for F1 racing vehicles. Although the final data collected may not be entirely accurate due to errors discussed in the paper, the ideas presented in this project can be fully realized with some minor changes and adjustments.

Conceptual Design Substantiation of an Ultra-Long-Haul Airliner Capable of High-Subsonic Cruise

Abstract:

This report details the design of the Defiance, an ultra-long-haul airliner designed to cruise at Mach 0.94, as well as the process of arriving at the final design. The target customers of the Defiance are Emirates, Qatar, and Etihad Airways, which collectively are often dubbed the “ME3.” The nearly 10% increase in cruise Mach number as compared to current ultra-long-haul airliners such as the Boeing 777 and the Airbus A350 is expected to decrease flight time by as much as 15.6% and passenger-specific fuel consumption at range by as much as 35.5%. The legacy aerodynamic estimation tools VORLAX and EDET were used heavily by the team over the course of the design process, and ModelCenter was used to run trade studies that allowed the team to optimize the design of the Defiance to the best of their ability.

Numerical Solutions to Small Disturbance Equations

Abstract:

The objective of this experiment was to find a solution for the subsonic Prandtl-Glauert (PG) Equation using a Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulation to find the pressure distribution and drag coefficient of a symmetrical airfoil in order to compare the results to an incompressible and transonic simulation. This was done by setting up a computational model for the airfoil comprising 4 boundaries to the experimental area and overlaying a mesh grid on this area. The boundary conditions and mesh points were then solved using a central differencing method to obtain the velocity potential over the airfoil, which was then used to find the pressure distribution and drag coefficients. The results of the experiment showed that the subsonic simulation compared well to the results of the incompressible and transonic simulations for Mach 0.3 proving its accuracy for the subsonic regime. However, at Mach 0.85, the results became inaccurate due to the presence of a shockwave which was is not accounted for in the PG equation. Because of this, the transonic simulation was shown to be more accurate at higher Mach numbers which was expected. Overall, this experiment showed the differences between the subsonic and transonic simulations and how the subsonic simulation has a limited application to Mach numbers below 0.8. This experiment is also a great example as to why computational methods of analysis can be much faster and maintain the same accuracy of experimental or analytical methods.

Aerospike Drag on a Blunt Body

Abstract:

The goal of this report is to showcase an experiment that was designed to measure how the addition of an aerospike effect the drag on a blunt body. Two specific variables, Mach number and the aerospike length to body diameter ratio, were tested and it was found that an increase in either variable decreased the drag acting on the body. The ratio variable had the bigger impact with huge drag reduction but also had a limit to its effectiveness. However, the Mach number appeared to have no such limit and increasing the Mach number beyond the range tested may show a further reduction in the drag. This was shown by the creation of a MATLAB function that quadratically interpolated between the Mach numbers and cubically interpolated between the length ratios. Due to this function is was found that the upper limit of the length ratio was 1.25 and that after this point, the drag started to increase again. A minimum drag coefficient of 0.46 was found at a Mach number of 3 and a length ratio of 1.25, which is much lower than the drag coefficient of 0.95 when there was no aerospike present. Pressure coefficient data was also analyzed and supported these findings showing a decrease in the pressure, equating to a decrease in drag, with the addition of an aerospike. However, this analysis only showed a pressure decrease up to a certain location on the body and that after this point the pressure data for all cases matched each other. This analysis showed that the aerospike is only able to decrease the drag on the front face and front fore section of the body. Applying what was found in the data analysis to a hypothetical missile design showed that the thrust required to operate the missile in cruise decreased by half with the addition of an aerospike. With all that being said, it is evident that an aerospike is an essential component to any supersonic vehicle due to the improved drag reduction it offers.

Compressible Converging-Diverging Nozzles

Abstract:

When investigating the characteristics of Converging-Diverging Nozzles, the relative change in density for a given change in pressure attributed to high-speed flows, otherwise known as compressibility, alters the attributes of the flow with respect to the area of the cross-section. A flow within the subsonic regime will inversely increase in velocity as the cross-section area decreases, however, a fluid at speeds within the supersonic regime will increase in velocity as the cross-section area increases. Converging-Diverging Nozzles such as the De-Laval Nozzle induce pressure ratios between the inlet and outlet that dictate whether the fluid flow will be subsonic, choked with a Mach number of 1, or supersonic; all of which generate varying performances qualities. In this experiment, the key objective is to explore the parameters and components of compressible nozzle flow by utilizing a nozzle- flow test rig equipped with numerous pressure gauges, inlet and outlet thermometers, and a mass-flow rotameter. In the first part of the experiment, the ambient pressure and temperature are collected, and the nozzle-flow test rig is powered on. In part two, the control is set by keeping the outlet pressure fixed while performing five trials with varying inlet pressures in an increasing order. On the other hand, part three requires the inlet pressure to be fixed at a specific gauge pressure while the outlet pressure is incrementally increased for a total of five trials. The mass flow rate, temperature, and pressure at each individual pressure gauge for all eight pressure taps are collected within each trial for both parts. Part one and two of the experiment revealed that specific inlet and outlet pressures combinations enabled pressure ratios to spike causing shocks to form. At locations 2.25-milimeters, 3- milimeters, and 4.5-milimeters, shocks are formed after the generated pressure ratios of roughly 0.5, 0.26, and 0.15 respectively. Further observation throughout this experiment revealed the varying behaviors of Mach number, stagnation pressure, and stagnation temperature after the throat of the nozzle depending on whether the fluid is within the subsonic or supersonic regime. The study of Converging-Diverging Nozzles and its properties are significant in order to engineer engines and propulsion systems for their intended purpose, whether they are designed to invoke or avoid shock waves.

Investigation of Shock Wave Speed Through Burst Pressure Ratios

Abstract:

Characterized by a relatively high Mach Number and turbulent nature, High-Speed flows differ from Low-Speed flows in that it incorporates compressibility and its subsequent effects. Considered a High-Speed flow, a flow within the Supersonic regime, which breaks the local speed of sound, often invoke the presence of a Normal Shock. A Normal Shock is characterized by its perpendicular or “normal” alignment with the direction of a High- Speed flow and is generated by the drastic difference in pressure causing the specific pressure ratios inherent to Shocks. In this specific laboratory experiment, the correlation between the pressures within the Driver and Driven sections of a Shock Tube are investigated along with its influence and impact to the speed of the produced Shock Wave. This experiment is conducted by utilizing different membrane materials to act as a barrier between the Driver and Driven sections. This barrier between the Diaphragm allows the Driver section to be pressurized while maintaining the atmospheric pressure within the Driven portion before the material ruptures causing a Normal Shock Wave. Both Static and Dynamic Pressure Transducers within the Shock Tube’s Driven section measure and collect data according to the pressure differences and changes throughout the Shock Tube. The measurements and collected data in the laboratory allow for the linear correlation between Shock Wave Pressure Ratio, Burst Pressure Ratio, and Shock Wave Speed to be established. Overall, the forementioned High-Speed concepts in regard to Normal Shock are critical in the thermodynamic and aerodynamic aspects of designing an effective jet engine for an aircraft’s intended purpose

Winglets and Drag Polars

Abstract:

The goal of this experiment is to determine the lift and drag properties of the Eppler 212 Airfoil when a series of winglets are installed at different Reynolds Numbers. This experiment includes the effects of pressure drag, friction drag, and induced drag. This experiment was accomplished by placing the Eppler 212 airfoil in a low-speed wind tunnel, after installing the winglet, and measuring the axial and normal forces acting on it for angles of attack ranging from -15 degrees to 25 degrees at a Reynolds Number of 100k and 200k. From this, lift-curve and drag polar plots were made for each winglet at each Reynolds Number. It was found that some of the winglets helped to improve the lift and drag properties, but some did not have much of an effect. The notable winglets include the angle variation winglets, the medium length winglet, and the top and middle winglets. These specific winglets improved the lift and lift to drag ratio the most while also decreasing drag, leading to the conclusion that they are useful for actual aircraft and flight.

Drag Polar for a Finite Wing

Abstract:

The goal of this experiment is the determine the lift and drag properties of the NACA 0012 at different Reynolds Numbers to ultimately obtain the lift-curve and drag polar for the airfoil. This lab will include the effects of pressure drag, friction drag, and induced drag, which is a result of downwash, in its calculations. This experiment was accomplished by placing the NACA 0012 airfoil in a low-speed wind tunnel and measuring the axial and normal forces acting on it for angles of attack ranging from -15 degrees to 15 degrees at a Reynolds Number of 50k, 100k, and 150k. It was found that as the Reynolds Number increased, the lift coefficient increased, the stall angle remained constant, and the coefficient of drag decreased. For the most part this matches expectations as similar trends are shown by Airfoil Tools [4] and Abbott’s The Theory of Wing Sections [5], although they have slightly different coefficient values. The only difference is that they show the stall angle to also increase as Reynolds Number increases. This most likely indicates that there was an error in this experiment, but the results are not totally invalid.

Velocity Wake Profiles and Drag

Abstract:

The goal of this experiment is to calibrate a hotwire anemometer and use it to obtain data for the velocity wake profiles of the NACA 0012 and NACA 4412 airfoils to ultimately calculate their parasitic coefficients of drag. The parasitic drag is the drag cause solely by the innate shape and viscous effects of the airfoil without considering the drag caused by pressure. This was accomplished by setting the airfoils at their zero-lift angle of attack in a low-speed wind tunnel and measuring the upstream and downstream velocities. The normalized velocity wake profiles were plotted and are shown to be accurate since there is an expected decrease in velocity around the airfoils. The coefficients of drag were then calculated for the airfoils to be 0.046292 for the NACA 0012 and 0.000438 for the NACA 4412. After comparing these values to other reputable sources which state that the NACA 0012 should be 0.01 and the NACA 4412 should be about 0.15, they were determined to be incorrect and must be the result of some error in the experiment.

Lift and Drag on an Airfoil

Abstract:

The goal of this experiment is to measure the pressure across the surfaces of two airfoils, the NACA 0012 and the NACA 4412, at various angles of attack by placing them in a low-speed wind tunnel to compare their different geometries. This pressure data will then be used to calculate the coefficients of pressure, the x force and z force coefficients, the lift and drag coefficients, and the lift-to-drag ratio of each airfoil at each angle of attack. The coefficients of pressure will be plotted to get the pressure distribution, the coefficients of lift and drag will be plotted to get the lift curve and the drag curve, and the lift-to-drag ratio will be plotted to determine the aerodynamic efficiency. The results show that the symmetrical NACA 0012 has a stall angle of 10 degrees and a zero-lift angle of 0 degrees, while the cambered NACA 4412 has a stall angle of 8 degrees and a zero-lift angle of -4 degrees. These results were validated by various sources to be accurate. The maximum lift-to-drag ratio of the NACA 0012 was found to be 100 at 4 degrees, while the maximum lift-to-drag ratio of the NACA 4412 was found to be 16.5 at 4 degrees. These results could not be validated and are most-likely incorrect.

Calculating Lift Using an Equivalent Line Vortex

Abstract:

The goal of this experiment is to calculate the lift per unit span of the NACA 0012 by modeling it as a vortex. The effect of this vortex is then canceled by mirrored vortices above and below the airfoil along an equivalent line, allowing the lift of the airfoil to be calculated using only the upper and lower surface pressure as well as the distance from the airfoil to the top of the wind tunnel test section. This method led to results consistent with those found from Airfoil Tools. The results were also compared to the results of Lab 2 although the results of that Lab are associated with a significant amount of error. In the end, the stall angle of the NACA 0012 was found to be 12 degrees, the maximum lift per unit span was found to be 78, and the maximum lift coefficient was found to be 1.25 by using this method of analysis. These results are extremely similar to the actual properties of the NACA 0012 airfoil validating the method of equivalent line vortexes.
